# Period-tracker users: basic needs, complaints and praises (Flo, Clue, Period Calendar, Stardust, My Calendar, Apple Cycle Tracking, Pepapp, beije and similar)

Research date: 2026-10-07. Material from 2023 to 2026 unless marked **[OLDER]**. Quotes are verbatim excerpts under 15 words. Turkish quotes have an English gloss in parentheses (the gloss is a translation, not a quote).

## 1. What do users complain about most? (ranked, with frequency signals, quotes and product requirements)

### Takeaway
The dominant complaint everywhere is monetization pressure on basic tracking: paywalls, upsell pop-ups and ads that stand between the user and her calendar. These are named in 58 to 62% of negative US reviews of Flo and Clue, and ads in 49% of negative Turkish Pepapp reviews. Next come: updates that break the app; loss of data or accounts; predictions that are wrong or jump around, with no way to correct them; billing and cancellation traps; and forced or broken logins. For Turkish Flo users the top complaint is English-only content (63% of negative reviews). Pregnancy loss, perimenopause, partner sharing and notification control come up less often but are severe when they do.

### Inferences
- **What drives anger.** Monetization pressure drives anger far more than missing features do. Feature-light, low-pressure apps get 2 to 8% 1-star reviews (My Calendar, ABISHKKING Period Calendar). Feature-rich, subscription-led apps get 23 to 31% (Flo, Clue, Stardust), and Pepapp reached 65% after it added heavy ads and a paywall.
- **Biggest switching barrier.** The biggest barrier to switching is years of history, and many long-time users of Flo, Clue and Pepapp now say they want to leave. Importing data from competitors is therefore an acquisition lever.
- **Privacy is a trust requirement.** It is rarely the explicit complaint (except for Stardust), but it is a top criterion when people choose an app (see Q3). It is also an obvious way to stand out, given Flo's legal history.

## 2. What do users praise, and what makes them stick with an app?

### Takeaway
Users stay for predictions that "hit the day", for years of reliable history they do not want to lose, and for a simple calendar-first screen. A free core without pressure to buy and gentle, useful reminders matter next. Secondary reasons are symptom and mood notes that help at the doctor, pregnancy tracking, playful customization (especially for teens), widgets, working backup, and a reputation for privacy (Clue, Apple).

### Inferences
- **Retention requirements, in order:**
  1. Predictions that feel accurate, with transparent uncertainty.
  2. History that is never lost, with backup, export and visible multi-year statistics. This turns "years of data" from a source of fear into a source of trust.
  3. A home screen of zero to one tap: today's cycle day and the countdown.
  4. A free core with no ad pressure, stated explicitly in the store listing.
  5. Gentle reminders the user can configure.
  6. A one-tap, doctor-ready summary (PDF) with dates, cycle lengths and symptoms.
  7. A home or lock-screen widget with a discreet countdown.
  8. Optional playful themes for younger users.
- **Missing field.** No mainstream app clearly offers a time-of-day field for bleeding start. A popular Turkish request asks for one (68 upvotes), and it is cheap to add.
- **Lost goodwill is a switching window.** Simple apps earn loyalty only while they stay restrained. Users of My Calendar and Simple Design already note that ads are increasing, so their loyal base may be open to switching.

### Gaps
- **No independent accuracy benchmark.** There is no independent benchmark comparing the prediction accuracy of these specific apps. "Accurate" in reviews is perceived accuracy.
- **No retention data.** No public retention or churn figures by app were found.
- **Widgets are under-measured.** Widget demand is visible only anecdotally.

## 3. What do academic and industry research say about user needs, abandonment, accuracy, and tracking to avoid or achieve pregnancy?

### Takeaway
Most people track simply to know when their next period will come. In a representative survey of five US states, 91% of app users track their cycle. About 1 in 5 also use the app to avoid pregnancy and about 1 in 6 to conceive. Research keeps finding the same unmet needs: accuracy for irregular cycles, the ability to correct data, privacy, transparency and control of data, and design that is not pink, stereotyped or focused on fertility. Privacy fears after the Roe v. Wade ruling made many users reconsider their app but did not cause mass abandonment.

### Cited Findings

#### How many people track, and why
- **US representative survey (5 states: AZ, IA, NJ, OH, WI).** Neiman et al., *Contraception*, 2025.
  - Use of period or fertility tracking technology rose from "over a third" of women before the Dobbs ruling to "nearly half" after it.
  - Motivations among app users, before and after Dobbs:

    | Motivation | Before Dobbs | After Dobbs |
    |---|---|---|
    | Track menstrual cycles | 91.2% | 91.7% |
    | Decide when to have sex to avoid pregnancy | 19.5% | 20.6% |
    | Decide when to have sex to become pregnant | 17.2% | 14.5% |
    | Decide whether to have sex without contraception | 11.2% | 11.5% |
    | Decide which contraceptive method to use | 3.8% | 3.5% |

  - Source: [Ohio Policy Evaluation Network brief, Neiman et al. 2025](https://open.osu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Neiman-et-al-2025.pdf)
- **UK poll (ICO, Sep 2023).**
  - A third of women have used apps to track periods or fertility.
  - When choosing an app, women ranked transparency about data use (59%) and data security (57%) above cost (55%) and ease of use (55%).
  - Over half of users believed they saw more baby or fertility adverts after signing up, and 17% found those ads distressing.
  - Source: [ICO press release, Sep 2023](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2023/09/ico-to-review-period-and-fertility-tracking-apps). The figures come from search-result summaries of this release and of legal-press coverage ([GRC Report](https://www.grcreport.com/post/are-women-concerned-about-data-security-when-it-comes-to-period-and-fertility-apps)); I did not open the release page itself.
- **US prevalence (KFF).** Nearly a third of US women use a period-tracking app. This is a KFF survey cited secondhand by Michigan Advance in May 2024; I could not open the primary KFF source. — [Michigan Advance, 2024](https://michiganadvance.com/2024/05/16/many-worry-about-data-privacy-on-period-apps-but-may-not-take-steps-to-protect-it-study-shows/)
- **2026 systematic review and meta-analysis.**
  - It pooled 14 studies (2017 to 2025, 3,547 participants).
  - Pooled prevalence of app use was 61% (95% CI 45–75%). After excluding high risk-of-bias studies it was 43%.
  - Uses: cycle monitoring, planning or avoiding pregnancy, symptoms, health information.
  - Concerns: "predictive accuracy, privacy, data security, and emotional distress from inaccurate predictions".
  - Source: [Nigerian Medical Journal 2026, via Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42605407)
- **Korea, Millennials and Gen Z (JMIR, 2024).** Of 700 women surveyed, 431 (62.3%) used period apps mainly to predict their next cycle. The authors call accuracy and literacy "an ongoing task". — [JMIR 2024;26:e53146](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53146)
- **What predicts tracking related to pregnancy (US, 249 users, 2026).** Older age, risk of unintended pregnancy, anxiety about pregnancy, and living in a state with an abortion ban predicted more frequent use. — [Health Informatics Journal 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/14604582261488920)
- **Biased sample example.** A 2023 survey reported that 72.8% of 368 respondents tracked mainly "to avoid getting pregnant". However, respondents were recruited partly through a Marquette Method (fertility-awareness method) instructor mailing list, and 96.7% used a fertility-awareness method. The figure should not be generalized. — [Medicina 2023, PubMed 37763628](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37763628/)
- **Scoping review of 58 papers (2014 to 2023).** People use reproductive-health apps for education, contraception and conception. The apps improve menstrual-health literacy but have limitations that adversely affect user experience. — [Healthcare 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13080877)

#### Why people stop using an app or switch
- **[OLDER, 2017] Epstein et al., CHI 2017** (687 survey respondents, 2,000 app reviews, 12 interviews, 9 apps).
  - Frustrations: inaccurate predictions for irregular cycles with no way to correct them; assumptions of a male partner and female identity; "pink, flowery" design instead of discreet design; no explanation of how predictions are made.
  - Recommendations: allow user correction, avoid stereotypes, use discreet design, enable data export, and adapt to changing life stages.
  - Source: [University of Washington News, 2017](https://washington.edu/news/2017/05/02/period-tracking-apps-failing-users-in-basic-ways-study-finds)
- **After Roe v. Wade.**
  - 38.3% of participants in a 2022 survey had reconsidered using a cycle-tracking app because of current events. This figure is from a search-result excerpt; I did not open the full article. — [mHealth (AME), post-Roe comfort study](https://mhealth.amegroups.org/article/view/118281/html)
  - In a CHI 2024 study of 183 US women, participants were significantly concerned about law-enforcement and third-party access, yet only 9% had acted on it, for example by deleting the app. They felt "uninformed and powerless". The article page returned 403, so these figures come from the search-result summary. — [Michigan Advance, 2024](https://michiganadvance.com/2024/05/16/many-worry-about-data-privacy-on-period-apps-but-may-not-take-steps-to-protect-it-study-shows/); [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1635407)
- **Cost.** A UK user-experience study, as summarized in search results, describes a participant who stopped after the free trial because of the cost-of-living crisis but would have continued if the app had been free. The full ICO report returned 404, so this is unverified. — [ICO user-experience report](https://cy.ico.org.uk/media/about-the-ico/documents/4029278/understanding-the-user-experience-of-fertility-and-period-tracking-apps.pdf)
- **What users want (qualitative, 25 users, 2025).** They prefer apps with good predictions and a better interface, want ownership of and access to their data, and want clearer privacy statements. — [Oxford Open Digital Health 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/oodh/oqaf011)
- **The same pattern in 2026 store reviews.** Long-time users are leaving because of new paywalls, ads and broken updates rather than because tracking has stopped being useful (see Q1, C5).

#### How accurate predictions are
- **University of Sydney, Oct 2024 (Copp and Ford, literature review).**
  - Only 13 to 16% of women have 28-day cycles, and only 13% ovulate on day 14.
  - In one study, cycle length varied by 5 or more days for more than half of women.
  - Many calendar-based apps still assume day-14 ovulation.
  - When apps were used for contraception, failure rates were 7.2 to 8.3%, but the underlying studies often excluded people whose cycle length varied.
  - The advice is to treat predictions as approximations.
  - Source: [University of Sydney, 2024](https://sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/10/02/can-you-trust-your-period-tracking-app-.html)
- **Calendar methods (2024 web-app study).** Calendar-based predictions work for regular cycles but are less reliable for irregular ones, because they depend on a fixed cycle-length range. The authors call for adaptive models. This is a small study in a minor journal, taken from the search summary of its abstract. — [UniMAP AMCI 2024](https://ejournal.unimap.edu.my/index.php/amci/article/view/1485)
- **Transparency.** Many apps record biometric data such as temperature or LH but do not use it in their predictions, and many do not explain how predictions are made. Apps are also described as most accurate for regular cycles, while the people who most need them have irregular ones. This comes from the search summary of a 2024 explainer apparently republished from the Copp and Ford article; I did not open the page. — [University of Newcastle, 2024](https://www.newcastle.edu.au/hippocampus/story/2024/can-i-trust-my-period-tracking-app)

#### Privacy research and enforcement
- **[OLDER, 2022] Mozilla.** 18 of 25 reproductive-health apps and devices got "Privacy Not Included" warnings. The reviewed apps included Flo, "Period Calendar Period Tracker" and "My Calendar Period Tracker". Mozilla also found no clear policies on sharing data with law enforcement. — [Mozilla Foundation](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/ca/privacynotincluded/articles/in-post-roe-v-wade-era-mozilla-labels-18-of-25-popular-period-and-pregnancy-tracking-tech-with-privacy-not-included-warning/)
- **Meta verdict, Aug 2025.** A jury found Meta violated California privacy law using Flo users' data; Flo and Google settled. — [Lawdragon](https://www.lawdragon.com/news-features/2025-08-25-big-tech-on-trial-jury-finds-meta-liable-for-misusing-women-health-data)

### Inferences
- **Who to design for.** Design first for "when is my next period, and is it late?", which is about 90% of use. Fertility (avoiding or achieving pregnancy) is a large minority of 15 to 20% each, and it needs a clear mode switch rather than being imposed on everyone.
- **Prediction honesty is a need in its own right.** Given real cycle variability, the app should show ranges or confidence and personal statistics such as average length and variation, and avoid presenting a single day as certain. The 2026 meta-analysis links inaccurate predictions to emotional distress.
- **Privacy is a choice criterion more than a reason to leave.** In the UK, transparency (59%) beat cost and ease of use, while only 9% of worried US users took action. Strong, simple privacy guarantees are therefore best used to win users at the decision point, through the store listing and onboarding.

### Gaps
- **KFF.** I could not open the primary KFF source, so the prevalence figure is secondhand.
- **ICO report.** The full ICO user-experience report (2023 to 2024) returned 404, so its detailed percentages are missing.
- **Accuracy benchmark.** No 2023 to 2026 peer-reviewed study benchmarked the prediction error of Flo, Clue, Simple Design, Stardust or My Calendar against each other.
- **Turkey.** No Turkish academic study on period-app use or abandonment was found; the Europe PMC search returned only noise.
- **Wearables.** Evidence on whether wearable or temperature-based predictions improve accuracy (Apple Watch, Oura) was not researched.

## 4. What do Turkish-speaking users say on the App Store and Google Play?

### Takeaway
Turkish users want the same basics but have four local pain points:
1. Global apps (Flo, Clue) are only partly Turkish. Articles, the AI assistant and push messages are in English, even for paying subscribers.
2. Prices and billing feel like a trap, with subscriptions of ₺60 to ₺640 and charges after cancellation.
3. The local leader, Pepapp, collapsed in user sentiment after its April 2026 update, which added heavy ads, a paywall, lost data and login failures. This leaves a large group of loyal users looking for an alternative.
4. Some users worry about data going abroad or to Meta.

No Turkish review in my sample asked a mainstream app to track prayer or fasting exemptions. Muslim-focused trackers that do this exist but have only 0 to 11 ratings in Turkey.

### Inferences
- **The clearest gap in the Turkish market.** A fully Turkish app (interface, content, assistant, notifications, support) with a free, ad-free core and honest TL pricing addresses the top complaint of Flo's Turkish users (language) and of Pepapp's users (ads and paywall) at the same time.
- **Pepapp's disaffected base is the largest acquisition pool.** Loyal users of 5 to 10 years are deleting the app. An "import from Pepapp" option, via screenshot or manual entry of past dates if Pepapp has no export, would let them switch without losing history.
- **Privacy messaging should be local and concrete.** For example, where the data is stored, that there is no Meta or Google tracking, and one-tap account deletion. Turkish users raise data going abroad and to Meta specifically.
- **Religious features are an untested hypothesis.**
  - There is no organic demand in reviews of mainstream apps, and dedicated apps have negligible traction. This suggests either that users don't expect period apps to handle it, or that the need is already met elsewhere (worship-tracker apps, or simply memory).
  - A low-cost, opt-in module could be tested, for example "count of fasting days missed during Ramadan" and private hayız day counts.
  - The precise bleeding start time that users already request would also support rulings based on a school of law's day limits, because Muslim trackers compute hayız versus istihaza from bleeding duration.
  - This should be validated with a survey before investing; it should not be a headline feature.
- **Teen-safe defaults are a requirement in Turkey.** That means no sexual content in partner features, no open forum, and age-appropriate articles.

### Gaps
- **Google Play depth.** Only the newest 150 reviews per app; older Turkish Google Play reviews and the Turkish local app "Adet Takvimi" (AZMED) could not be retrieved.
- **No Turkish community discussions.** Ekşi Sözlük, kadinlarkulubu and Şikayetvar threads on period apps were not reachable or were drowned out by the FLO shoe brand.
- **No independent price benchmark.** There is no independent data on what Turkish users would pay. Price sensitivity is inferred from reviews only.
- **KVKK not researched.** Specific requirements of Turkey's data protection law (KVKK) for health data were not researched; a legal review is needed.
- **Pepapp update details not found.** No news article was found explaining Pepapp's April 2026 v3.0 change.
- **The Meta claim about Pepapp could not be verified or refuted.**
