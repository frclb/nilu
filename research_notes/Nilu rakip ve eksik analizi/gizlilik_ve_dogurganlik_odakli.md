# Privacy-first, fertility-focused and niche cycle-tracking apps: Natural Cycles, Ovia, Glow, Euki, drip, Spot On, Premom, Daysy, Kindara, Clearblue, Bellabeat, Moody Month and wearables, with lessons for Nilu

Compiled 7 October 2026. App Store data comes from US listings fetched on that date. Older information is dated inline. Flo, Clue, Period Calendar, Stardust and Apple Cycle Tracking appear only for contrast, because another researcher covers them.

---

## 1. Natural Cycles: FDA/CE status, temperature sources, pricing, accuracy evidence and controversies

### Takeaway
Natural Cycles (NC) is the only app in this group that is regulated as a contraceptive. It received a CE mark as a Class IIb device in February 2017 and an FDA De Novo authorization in August 2018, and by July 2026 it had six FDA clearances. It has turned that regulatory position into a temperature platform that works with any device: its own thermometer and NC° Band, Apple Watch, Oura, Garmin and WHOOP. The headline 93% typical-use and 98% perfect-use figures come mostly from company-authored real-world data, and NC has a history of regulatory and advertising scrutiny. It is a premium product ($21.99 a month or $149.99 a year on the US App Store) that requires an account, and its privacy protection is pseudonymization in the cloud ("Go Anonymous"), not on-device storage.

### Cited Findings
**Regulatory timeline**
- February 2017: TÜV SÜD certified Natural Cycles as a Class IIb medical device for contraception. It was the first app certified as a contraceptive in the EU (CE0123). — [Wikipedia: Natural Cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Cycles); [SBS News, 9 Feb 2017](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/2017/02/09/app-certified-contraception-device); [Mobile Marketing Magazine](https://mobilemarketingmagazine.com/fertility-app-receives-clinical-approval-as-a-contraceptive/)
- August 2018: the FDA permitted marketing of NC as the first mobile medical app for contraception. It reviewed clinical studies covering 15,570 women who used the app for an average of about eight months. The failure rate was 1.8% with perfect use and 6.5% with typical use. "Typical use" includes unprotected sex on days the app showed "use protection". — [FDA press announcement (2018)](https://www.fda.gov/NewsEvents/Newsroom/PressAnnouncements/ucm616511.htm); [pharmaphorum](https://pharmaphorum.com/news/contraception-app-natural-cycles-approved)
- By July 2026 NC had six FDA clearances:
  - 2018: De Novo (birth control app)
  - 2021: 510(k) for the Oura Ring integration
  - 2023: 510(k) for the Apple Watch integration
  - 2024: 510(k) for a Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) covering future hardware
  - 2025: 510(k) for over-the-counter and prescription availability
  - 2026: 510(k) for a "next-generation" algorithm

  The 2026 algorithm "combines statistical modeling with machine learning" to increase the number of "Green Days" (days when protection is not needed). NC says it performs better with irregular cycles and lower-quality temperature data and personalizes faster. — [Contemporary OB/GYN, 27 Jul 2026](https://www.contemporaryobgyn.net/view/natural-cycles-receives-fda-clearance-for-ai-powered-fertility-algorithm)
- The Apple Watch integration (Series 8 and Ultra) received FDA 510(k) clearance and clearance "from European regulators". NC claims the same 93% typical and 98% perfect effectiveness as with a basal thermometer or Oura Ring. — [BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/natural-cycles-receives-fda-clearance-to-integrate-its-birth-control-app-with-data-measured-by-apple-watch); [FemTech Insider](https://femtechinsider.com/natural-cycles-apple-watch-integration/); [NC: Apple Watch page](https://www.naturalcycles.com/apple-watch)

**Temperature sources and wearables**
- Oura: cleared in 2021 and launched on 2 August 2022. — [BusinessWire, 2 Aug 2022](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220802005338/en/Natural-Cycles-is-Now-Compatible-with-the-Oura-Ring); [Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-partners-with-fda-cleared-birth-control-app-natural-cycles/)
- NC° Band: NC's own wearable, worn only at night. Its launch was supported by the 2024 PCCP clearance. — [The Flow Space (search snippet; page redirected to a paywall/tollbit)](https://www.theflowspace.com/physical-health/prevention-longevity/natural-cycles-nc-band-hormonal-health-3007176/)
- Garmin (31 March 2026): works with fēnix 8, Forerunner 570/970, Venu 4 and Venu X1. Overnight skin temperature syncs to NC each morning to give a daily fertility status "either for pregnancy prevention or planning". — [FemTech Insider, 31 Mar 2026](https://femtechinsider.com/garmin-integrates-with-natural-cycles-to-turn-skin-temperature-data-into-fertility-insights/)
- WHOOP (4 August 2026): works with WHOOP MG and 5.0. Eligible new members get a free 12-month NC subscription. WHOOP said its female membership grew 111% year over year. — [FemTech Insider, 4 Aug 2026](https://femtechinsider.com/whoop-partners-with-natural-cycles-for-hormone-free-birth-control/); [BusinessWire release, Aug 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260804399367/en/)

**Product, pricing and reception (US App Store, fetched 7 Oct 2026)**
- Listing: "Natural Cycles: Fertility App", seller NaturalCycles Nordic AB, rated 4.8★ from about 30K ratings. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/natural-cycles-birth-control/id765535549)
- Pricing: free download with in-app purchases of $21.99 a month or $149.99 a year. — same source
- Modes: NC° Birth Control, Plan Pregnancy, Follow Pregnancy, Perimenopause and Postpartum. — same source
- Devices: NC° Band, Apple Watch Series 8+, Oura, WHOOP, Garmin and a basal thermometer. LH test strips can also be logged. — same source
- Languages: English, Portuguese, Spanish and Swedish only (no Turkish). Age rating 16+. Version 5.9.1 was released 29 September. — same source
- Privacy label: "Contact Info" is used to track. Health & Fitness, Sensitive Info, Identifiers, Usage Data, Purchases and Contact Info are linked to the user's identity. — same source
- Review themes: body autonomy, better mood after stopping hormonal contraception, and an intuitive, personalized design. — same source
- Pricing conflict: help-centre and third-party summaries show $9.99 a month and $79.99 a year, sometimes with the NC° Band free on the annual plan, and an "NC° Bundle" with thermometer at $99.99 a year. This does not match the App Store prices. It may reflect web versus in-app pricing, promotions or older prices. — [NC Help Center (403 when fetched; search snippet)](https://help.naturalcycles.com/hc/en-us/articles/9128446250013-How-does-the-Natural-Cycles-subscription-work-); [thepricer.org](https://www.thepricer.org/how-much-does-natural-cycles-cost)

**Accuracy evidence and how independent it is**
- NC's company-run 2017 study of more than 22,000 women was the basis for the 93% typical-use figure that Sweden's Medical Products Agency (MPA) accepted. — [Digital Health, Sept 2018](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2018/09/swedish-authorities-natural-cycles-app-assessment/)
- Evidence summaries put the typical-use Pearl Index at about 6.9 in the study of 22,785 women. They note that the studies were mostly done by NC or affiliated researchers, with authors from NC's own medical communications department, and that no study compares NC with other contraceptives. — [NICE MIB244 "Clinical and technical evidence"](https://www.nice.org.uk/advice/mib244/chapter/Clinical-and-technical-evidence) (403 when fetched; content taken from search-engine summary, so treat with caution)
- NC's large cycle-characteristics paper (Bull et al., 2019) is authored by NC employees and founders. — [Bull et al., npj Digital Medicine 2019 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710244/)

**Controversies (2018)**
- Stockholm's Södersjukhuset hospital reported that 37 of 668 women seeking abortion between September and December 2017 had relied on NC. The case was referred to the Swedish MPA. The MPA closed its investigation in September 2018, finding the rate consistent with 93% typical-use effectiveness, but asked NC to state the risk of unwanted pregnancy more clearly in its instructions for use and in the app. — [Digital Health, Sept 2018](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2018/09/swedish-authorities-natural-cycles-app-assessment/); [Engadget, 15 Sep 2018](https://www.engadget.com/2018-09-15-natural-cycles-investigation-sweden.html)
- August 2018: the UK Advertising Standards Authority upheld complaints that a Facebook ad calling the app "highly accurate" was misleading. — [ASA ruling A17-393896](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/naturalcycles-nordic-ab-sweden-a17-393896.html); [Digital Health](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2018/09/swedish-authorities-natural-cycles-app-assessment/)

**Privacy positioning**
- In 2022, after Roe v. Wade was overturned, CEO Elina Berglund Scherwitzl promised an anonymous mode that would be "impossible for us to connect the personal data and the sensitive data". NC also committed to never selling data, getting third-party (Mozilla) review, and working with legal counsel. — [Live 5 News, 4 Jul 2022](https://www.live5news.com/2022/07/04/online-health-data-could-be-used-prosecute-abortion-experts-say/); [YPulse, 27 Jun 2022](https://www.ypulse.com/newsfeed/2022/06/27/menstrual-tracking-apps-are-trying-to-make-their-users-data-anonymous-following-the-overturn-of-roe-v-wade/); [NC response to BHRRC survey (PDF)](https://www.business-humanrights.org/documents/38278/Response_from_Natural_Cycles.pdf)
- Today this exists as "Go Anonymous", part of the "NC° Secure" program included in every subscription. It separates identifying information from fertility data so that "no one — not even Natural Cycles — can link your data to you". NC° Secure also covers encryption and pseudonymization. — [NC Help Center: What is Go Anonymous](https://help.naturalcycles.com/hc/en-us/articles/20116990594333-What-is-Go-Anonymous) (search snippet; page not fetched)

### Inferences
- NC's advantage over competitors is regulatory: a CE Class IIb mark, six FDA clearances and a large proprietary temperature dataset. Since 2024 its strategy has been to accept temperature from any device and to sell through wearable makers (WHOOP bundles a year of NC; Garmin integration). A basic on-device tracker cannot credibly compete on contraception, and in the EU and probably Turkey any "safe day" or "green day" claim would make the product a medical device. Nilu should avoid contraception language completely.
- The 2026 algorithm update's stated goals (better handling of irregular cycles and noisy data) show that even the market leader treats irregular cycles as the main prediction problem.
- NC's privacy model (cloud account plus opt-in pseudonymization) is weaker in structure than "data never leaves the device". Nilu can make the stronger claim honestly, provided it adds no analytics or crash SDKs.
- At about $150 a year, NC sets the ceiling of what users will pay for contraception-grade service. A tracker without contraception claims cannot charge that.
- NC has no Turkish UI, which leaves room for a Turkish-first app among Turkish users who want fertility awareness tools.

### Gaps
- NC° Band launch date and standalone price could not be verified (the article was behind a paywall redirect).
- I found no NC user numbers or revenue for 2025–2026.
- I did not verify whether NC is sold in Turkey.
- I found no independent, non-company effectiveness study published since 2020. The "How the First FDA-Approved Contraception App Performs Across Populations" study ([Chief Healthcare Executive](https://chiefhealthcareexecutive.com/view/how-the-first-fdaapproved-contraception-app-performs-across-populations)) was not read.
- NC's marketing calls it "the only FDA-cleared birth control app". There was an earlier dispute with another cycle app over FDA clearance claims ([IoT M2M Council](https://iotm2mcouncil.org/iot-library/news/connected-health-news/fda-clearance-triggers-row-between-cycle-tracking-apps/)) that I did not examine. Clue's clearance is covered by another researcher.

## 2. Ovia and Glow: free-with-ads and employer/insurer models, data practices, community, reception

### Takeaway
Ovia and Glow are early-2010s fertility, pregnancy and parenting suites. Their consumer apps are free and earn money indirectly:
- **Ovia** sells to employers and health plans (B2B2C) and has been owned by Labcorp since August 2021.
- **Glow** earns from advertising, tracking and data-driven marketing, plus a premium subscription and large community forums.

Both carry documented privacy problems:
- Ovia's employer dashboards (Washington Post, 2019)
- Glow's $250,000 settlement with the California Attorney General (2020)
- Mozilla "Privacy Not Included" warnings for both (2022)
- current App Store labels showing health and sensitive data linked to identity

Both still have high ratings (4.7–4.8★), but both US listings were last updated in September 2024, which suggests the consumer apps are in maintenance mode.

### Cited Findings
**Ovia**
- Labcorp announced the acquisition of Ovia Health on 12 August 2021; terms were not disclosed. At the time Ovia had more than 2,000 employer customers, about $20M in annual revenue and had supported "more than 15 million family journeys". — [MedCity News, Aug 2021](https://medcitynews.com/2021/08/labcorp-buys-fertility-benefits-company-ovia/); [Labcorp newsroom](https://www.labcorp.com/newsroom/labcorp-extends-leadership-womens-health-acquisition-ovia-health); [Labcorp 8-K exhibit (SEC)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/920148/000092014821000053/form81221ex991oviaacquisit.htm)
- Ovia sells to employers as part of a family-benefits platform (fertility and family-building benefits, care management). For health plans it offers remote monitoring, dynamic assessments, symptom monitoring and analytics "to identify risks early and navigate members to case management". — same sources
- In April 2019 the Washington Post reported that employers paying Ovia per employee could offer a special version of the app that sent "de-identified", aggregated data to an employer dashboard. The data included how many staff had high-risk pregnancies or premature births, the top medical questions researched, and return-to-work timelines. Experts warned that women in small workplaces could be re-identified and that the data could influence benefit costs. — [Boston Globe (WaPo story), 10 Apr 2019](https://www.bostonglobe.com/business/2019/04/10/menstrual-monitoring-app-raises-questions-about-privacy/bRdtxn094ZO9ImN0zlKBuN/story.html); [The Daily Beast](https://thedailybeast.com/pregnancy-tracking-apps-share-data-with-users-employers-report)
- Ovia is still sold as a health-plan benefit in 2026, for example in University of Texas System "UT SELECT" materials. — [UT System, Ovia Health overview (2026 PDF)](https://www.utsystem.edu/sites/default/files/documents/publication/2026/ut-select-ovia-health-overview/ovia-health.pdf)
- US App Store (fetched 7 Oct 2026): "Ovia Cycle & Pregnancy Tracker" by Ovuline, Inc. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ovia-cycle-pregnancy-tracker/id570244389)
  - Rated 4.8★ from 91K ratings; free.
  - "Ovia+" comes through an employer or health plan and adds coaching, messaging with health professionals and condition programs.
  - Covers fertility, ovulation and period tracking, pregnancy, postpartum, and perimenopause/menopause, with more than 2,000 articles.
  - Integrates with Apple Health and Fitbit. Has an optional PIN and "Friends & Family Sharing".
  - Privacy label: health & fitness, location, contact info, identifiers and "sensitive info" are linked to the user, with the developer's advertising/marketing among the listed purposes.
  - Latest version 8.10.0, dated 24 September 2024.
- Mozilla's August 2022 review gave "Ovia Pregnancy" a *Privacy Not Included warning (according to a search summary of Mozilla's materials). — [Mozilla, Aug 2022](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/research/library/in-post-roe-v-wade-era-mozilla-labels-18-of-25-popular-period-and-pregnancy-tracking-tech-with-privacy-not-included-warning/)

**Glow (Glow, Eve by Glow, Nurture, Baby)**
- 17 September 2020: the California Attorney General announced a settlement with Glow, Inc. over "serious privacy and basic security failures". It included a $250,000 civil penalty, and the alleged violations were of CMIA, UCL and FAL, with no admission of liability. Glow had to run an information security program and get affirmative authorization before sharing data with third parties. A first-of-its-kind term required Glow to consider how privacy and security lapses uniquely affect women. — [CA AG release (HHS-OIG repost)](https://oig.hhs.gov/fraud/enforcement/attorney-general-becerra-announces-landmark-settlement-against-glow-inc-fertility-app-risked-exposing-millions-of-womens-personal-and-medical-information); [WilmerHale, 29 Sep 2020](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20200929-california-settles-with-glow-app-over-alleged-privacy-and-security-violations)
- 9 August 2022: Mozilla gave *Privacy Not Included warnings to "Glow & Eve by Glow" and "Glow Nurture & Glow Baby". Glow claimed it did not share data with third parties, but its privacy policy allowed sharing with advertisers, business partners and professional advisors, and also with law enforcement, including through "voluntary disclosure". — [Mozilla PNI: Glow & Eve by Glow](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/privacynotincluded/glow-eve-by-glow/); [Mozilla PNI: Glow Nurture & Glow Baby](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/privacynotincluded/glow-nurture-glow-baby/)
- US App Store (fetched 7 Oct 2026): "Glow Ovulation & Period App" by Glow, Inc. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/glow-ovulation-period-app/id638021335)
  - Rated 4.7★ from about 75K ratings.
  - In-app purchases: Glow Premium $15.99–$69.99 (quarterly and yearly) and "Glow Ovulation Calculator" $35.99.
  - "Data Used to Track You": purchases, location, contact info, identifiers and usage data. Data linked to the user also includes health & fitness, sensitive info, photos/videos and search history.
  - Features: BBT and cervical-mucus logging, community, Apple Health.
  - Latest version 12.1.1 (28 September 2024) added "Glow Coach", an AI companion with voice check-ins.
  - Review themes: doubts about ovulation-prediction accuracy, a slow app, aggressive premium upselling and privacy concerns. Users value the community and historical logging.
- Glow Premium unlocks an advanced cycle chart, cycle analysis, comparative insights, forecasts, an advanced BBT chart, premium articles and private messaging. The yearly plan is $69.99. — [AppFollow listing](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/glow-ovulation-period-app/638021335?country=us)

### Inferences
- In both models the money comes from someone other than the user (employers, advertisers, marketing partners), which gives the company a reason to collect identifiable data. Their privacy labels are the opposite of Euki's "Data Not Collected" label (Section 3).
- Ratings of 4.7–4.8★ show that users reward features (community, content, pregnancy tools, BBT charts) and rarely punish data practices in ratings. Privacy alone will not win users; it has to come with equally good everyday UX.
- Ovia's employer and insurer channel depends on US employer-funded fertility benefits and is not a ready template for Turkey.
- Glow's community is a key reason trying-to-conceive users stay, but forums need servers and moderation, which conflicts with a local-only design. Nilu could offer curated, offline educational content instead of forums.
- Both US listings were last updated in September 2024. Fertility charting from that generation may be under-served now, so there could be an opening for a modern, private BBT/LH logging experience.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 user numbers for Ovia's or Glow's consumer apps.
- Not confirmed whether Ovia's consumer app shows third-party ads.
- Glow's ownership and corporate status in 2025–2026 not verified.
- Eve by Glow ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1002275138)) not analyzed.
- Mozilla's July 2026 study did not include Ovia or Glow, so their current network behaviour is untested.
- App Store privacy-label details were taken from automated page extraction and should be checked by hand.

## 3. Euki and drip (and similar local-only, no-account, open-source apps): approach, adoption, limitations, user reviews

### Takeaway
Euki and drip are the reference designs for "no account, data stays on the device, no trackers, open source". Every independent privacy review rewards them: Euki was the only app to score 10/10 in Mozilla's July 2026 test, and both are on Consumer Reports' list of privacy-focused apps. Both remain small:
- Euki: about 90k downloads after Dobbs and about 10k active users in 2024; 157 ratings on the US App Store.
- drip: estimated tens of thousands of Android installs.

Their main limitations are thin features (Euki has no temperature tracking), manual-only data migration, no sync, limited languages and nonprofit funding. These are the gaps a polished privacy-first app could fill.

### Cited Findings
**Euki**
- Euki was built after research with Women Help Women and covers abortion, miscarriage, STIs and contraception. It has no back-end data collection. Secondary summaries of Mozilla's material date the release to 2019. Ibis says it was released "in partnership with Women Help Women in 2018", so the sources conflict on the year. — [Mozilla blog, 18 Nov 2022](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/privacy-not-included-spotlight-euki-on-how-it-gets-privacy-right/); [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/education/mozilla-tested-six-period-tracker-apps-only-one-earned-a-perfect-privacy-score/); [Ibis Reproductive Health, Jun 2024](https://www.ibisreproductivehealth.org/news/euki-launches-dedicated-independent-501c3-nonprofit-tech-organization)
- Design features: data stored on the device, customizable interface labels, automatic data deletion, password protection, and a decoy password that shows a false screen. The developers acknowledge these limits:
  - risk if the phone is stolen, and passwords can be cracked
  - renaming the app icon needs third-party tools
  - searching "Euki" reveals what the app is for
  - Euki is not covered by HIPAA

  Usage "increased exponentially" after the leaked Dobbs draft in May 2022. — [Mozilla blog, 18 Nov 2022](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/privacy-not-included-spotlight-euki-on-how-it-gets-privacy-right/)
- June 2024: Euki became an independent 501(c)(3) nonprofit. It had more than 90,000 downloads since Dobbs and 10,000+ active users. — [Ibis Reproductive Health](https://www.ibisreproductivehealth.org/news/euki-launches-dedicated-independent-501c3-nonprofit-tech-organization)
- After the Roe decision, Euki ran a public awareness campaign. — [LBB Online](https://www.lbbonline.com/news/secure-sexual-health-app-euki-launches-awareness-campaign-in-wake-of-roe-v-wade)
- US App Store (fetched 7 Oct 2026): seller Euki, Inc. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/euki/id1469213846)
  - Rated 4.2★ from only 157 ratings; free.
  - Privacy label: "Data Not Collected".
  - Available in English and Spanish only. Version 1.9.5, dated 4 September 2025.
  - Features: PIN with decoy mode, auto-delete, cycle predictions, abortion and contraception content, and a "Care Navigator" (beta).
  - Reviews praise its privacy and its predictions improving over time. Users ask for more tracking options (pain types, pad absorbency, late pills) and reported cycle-calculation bugs that were later fixed.
- Netzpolitik (16 Sep 2025): Euki cannot track body temperature and supports only manual data migration. Its emergency PIN "0000" shows false data if someone searches the phone. — [netzpolitik.org](https://netzpolitik.org/2025/datenschutzfreundliche-perioden-apps-zyklus-tracking-ohne-tracking/)
- Mozilla (16 July 2026): Euki scored 10/10, the only perfect score among six apps tested. It stores data locally and needs no account. Mozilla's summary line was "Data minimization is stronger than a promise not to misuse data." The summary also describes Euki as open source and nonprofit. — [Captain Compliance summary](https://captaincompliance.com/education/mozilla-tested-six-period-tracker-apps-only-one-earned-a-perfect-privacy-score/); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-study-ranks-euki-as-the-most-private-period-tracker/); [Mozilla report (403 when fetched)](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/nothing-personal/period-ovulation-trackers/)

**drip**
- drip is built by the Bloody Health collective, founded in Berlin in 2018 by programmer Marie Kochsiek as an alternative to apps that collect data. It is open source, works only locally (no servers store user data) and uses the sympto-thermal method. Its design and language are gender-neutral, and its motto is "Your data, your choice". — [SUPERRR Feminist Tech Fellowship](https://superrr.net/feministtech/fellow/drip/); [futurezone.at](https://futurezone.at/apps/drip-open-source-alternative-unter-den-menstruations-apps/401167623)
- From drip's official site:
  - "Everything you enter stays on your device"; "not another cute, pink app".
  - The app is "transparent in its calculations and encourages you to think for yourself".
  - Users can track just their period or also detect fertility.
  - Available on Google Play, F-Droid, as a direct APK, and on iOS. Current version 1.2411.20.
  - Funded by Germany's BMBF, the Prototype Fund, the Open Knowledge Foundation and the Mozilla Foundation. Contributions come through GitLab.

  — [dripapp.org](https://dripapp.org/)
- Netzpolitik (Sep 2025): drip covers cycle phases, symptom logging, sympto-thermal tracking with temperature, and forecasts. Data transfer is manual, but converters exist for Flo and Clue exports. — [netzpolitik.org](https://netzpolitik.org/2025/datenschutzfreundliche-perioden-apps-zyklus-tracking-ohne-tracking/)
- Third-party estimates for drip on Android are about 78.3K installs and about 4.8K monthly active users. These are estimates, not official figures. — [AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/com.drip); [Similarweb](https://similarweb.com/app/google/com.drip)

**Similar apps and independent shortlists**
- Periodical (Android, open source, by Arno Welzel) uses the Knaus-Ogino calendar method, backs up to an SD card and collects no data. Netzpolitik notes it is less precise for avoiding pregnancy. — [netzpolitik.org](https://netzpolitik.org/2025/datenschutzfreundliche-perioden-apps-zyklus-tracking-ohne-tracking/)
- Consumer Reports' Digital Lab examined four apps recommended by privacy advocates (Drip, Euki, Lady Cycle, Periodical). It checked whether they store data only on the device and whether they avoid third-party trackers. — [Consumer Reports (paywalled; date not confirmed)](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/health-privacy/period-tracker-apps-privacy-a2278134145/)

### Inferences
- Local-only design earns trust and expert endorsement but not mass adoption. Euki has 157 US App Store ratings after more than six years, compared with 75–91K for Glow and Ovia. Without polish, features, localization and marketing budgets, privacy-first apps stay niche.
- The common weak points to fix:
  - backup, restore and moving to a new phone (data loss is a frequent complaint about local apps; see Spot On reviews in Section 4)
  - import from Flo, Clue or Apple Health
  - optional BBT and LH logging
  - prediction quality for irregular cycles
  - languages beyond English, Spanish and German
- Euki's decoy PIN and auto-delete fit a threat model where a partner, parent or family member may check the phone, which is highly relevant in Turkey (see Section 6). They should be treated as core safety features, not gimmicks.
- drip's "transparent calculations" approach is a model for presenting predictions honestly (see Section 7).
- Nonprofit funding (grants, donations) explains the slow development. A sustainable paid model without ads is a gap neither app fills.

### Gaps
- No official download numbers for drip; its iOS App Store rating was not retrieved.
- Euki's Android rating and installs were not retrieved.
- Consumer Reports' evaluation date and detailed results are behind a paywall.
- I could not verify Euki's open-source repository directly; that claim comes from the Mozilla 2026 summary.

## 4. Other notable niche apps and devices: Spot On, Premom, Daysy, Kindara, Clearblue Connected, Bellabeat, Moody Month, and wearable temperature (Oura, Garmin, WHOOP)

### Takeaway
The rest of the niche falls into four groups:
- **Test-strip and hardware fertility tools:** Premom (Easy@Home LH strips with a camera-based AI reader), Daysy, Kindara/Wink, and Clearblue Connected (now discontinued).
- **Wearables that turn overnight skin temperature into cycle insights:** Oura's Fertile Window, and Garmin and WHOOP through NC.
- **Cycle-syncing wellness subscriptions:** Bellabeat and Moody Month.
- **Nonprofit trackers:** Spot On.

## 5. Which features address basic needs (simple logging, reliable predictions for irregular cycles, privacy, no forced account) versus advanced needs (contraception, fertility charting)?

### Takeaway
No app in this group combines all of the following:
- polished, fast logging
- honest predictions that adapt to irregular cycles
- no account and data only on the device
- local-language support (Turkish)

The basic-privacy apps (Euki, drip, Spot On) get privacy right but are weak on polish, predictions, backup and localization. Contraception and fertility-charting needs are served by account-based cloud products, often tied to hardware: NC, Premom, Glow, Ovia, Oura, Kindara and Daysy. User research and regulator polls show that predictions that cope with irregular cycles, clean UI, data ownership and transparency are the core "basic needs". Transparency and security rank above cost and ease of use when choosing an app.

### Inferences
**Basic needs, and who meets them**
1. **Fast logging.** Every app has it. Users find daily logging tedious (Mohan & Jenkins), so one-tap logging and "only log what you want" (drip's period-only mode) matter.
2. **Predictions that cope with irregular cycles.** Calendar-only apps do this poorly (Section 7). Spot On explicitly limits predictions to regular periods, and Euki reviews mention earlier calculation bugs. Only temperature-based products (NC, Oura) show measurable gains.
3. **Privacy and no forced account.** Euki, drip and Spot On meet this; NC, Glow, Ovia and Premom do not.
4. **Birth-control and pill reminders.** Spot On is strongest here.
5. **Discreet use (lock, decoy).** Euki leads; Ovia has an optional PIN.
6. **Backup and portability.** Weak in all local apps (manual migration in Euki and drip; data-loss complaints for Spot On).

**Advanced needs**
- Contraception: only NC, which is regulated.
- Fertility charting (BBT, LH, cervical mucus): drip, Glow, Kindara, Premom, Daysy.
- Wearable temperature: NC, Oura, Garmin and WHOOP through NC.
- Pregnancy, postpartum and perimenopause modes: NC, Ovia, Moody Month.
- Clinician or coaching access: Ovia+.
- Community: Glow.
- Cycle-syncing wellness programs: Moody Month, Bellabeat.

**What this means for Nilu**
- Nilu can win "basic needs plus privacy" in Turkish by matching Euki-level privacy with mainstream polish.
- It can then add some advanced features without regulatory claims: optional manual BBT and LH logging, Apple Watch temperature read on the device, and pregnancy mode.

- I found no survey quantifying how many Turkish users need contraception versus basic tracking or trying-to-conceive tools.
- I found no academic study comparing the accuracy of *period-start* predictions across the named apps.

## 6. How did apps market privacy after Roe v. Wade (2022), and how relevant is that positioning outside the US (EU, Turkey)?

### Takeaway
After the leaked draft (May 2022) and the Dobbs decision (June 2022), commercial apps marketed privacy mainly through promises: "anonymous modes" (Flo, NC), encryption and pseudonymization, never selling data, and clearer law-enforcement policies. Nonprofits (Euki, drip) pointed to their architecture: no server, no account. Independent testers have consistently rewarded architecture over promises:
- Mozilla 2022: 18 of 25 reproductive-health products received warnings.
- Mozilla 2026: Euki 10/10, Stardust 2/10.
- A 2026 academic study found explicit health-data leakage in 5 of 20 Android fertility apps.

Outside the US the threat is less about prosecution and more about ad-tech profiling, data breaches, and family or partner surveillance. The positioning still matters:
- EU and UK law treats cycle data as "special category" data, but enforcement gaps and harmful advertising persist (Cambridge 2025, ICO 2023).
- Poland's 2022 pregnancy registry showed that state-surveillance fears are not only American.
- In Turkey, health and sexual-life data are special-category data under KVKK (reformed in 2024). Abortion is legal to 10 weeks but in 2024 only 5.1% of public hospitals provided it, under pronatalist policy. Users also report strong worry that others can see what they do on their phones.

## 7. Evidence on the accuracy of app period and ovulation predictions, and implications for honest communication

### Takeaway
Calendar-only predictions are inherently imprecise:
- Only 13% of cycles are exactly 28 days long.
- Ovulation timing varies widely: the follicular phase has a 95% range of 10–30 days, while the luteal phase is relatively stable.
- Apps rarely predict the exact ovulation day: 8% were exactly right in one 10-app test, 67% were 2–9 days early, and only 4 of 53 sites and apps gave an accurate fertile window in 2016.

Physiological markers (BBT, wearable temperature, LH tests) clearly improve ovulation estimates: Oura's error was ±1.26 days against ±3.44 for calendar, and 1.48 against 6.63 days in irregular cycles. Honest communication therefore means showing ranges and uncertainty, labelling fertile windows as estimates, explaining the method, and never implying contraceptive reliability.

### Cited Findings
- Setton et al., *Obstetrics & Gynecology* (July 2016): of the 53 top websites and apps that predict a fertile window, only 4 (1 website and 3 apps: iPeriod, My Days, Clue) predicted it accurately. — [MedicalXpress, Jun 2016](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2016-06-websites-apps-accurate-fertile-window.html); [PROGRESS Educational Trust](https://www.progress.org.uk/study-finds-most-fertility-tracker-apps-are-inaccurate/)
- Worsfold, Marriott, Johnson & Harper, *Women's Health* (2021) tested 10 apps against five simulated cycle profiles. — [PMC8504278](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8504278/)
  - For regular cycles, 3 of 36 ovulation predictions (8%) were exactly right, and 67% were 2–9 days early.
  - Cycle-length predictions were good only for consistent 28-day cycles; for other patterns they were 0–8 days off.
  - Most apps showed a fixed 7-day fertile window regardless of the user (others used 6–16 days).
  - The authors say apps should "stop giving women inaccurate predictions for ovulation". Ovulation can only be predicted with a marker: BBT, LH tests or cervical mucus.
- Bull et al., *npj Digital Medicine* (2019), was written by NC-affiliated authors using NC data. — [PMC6710244](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710244/)
  - Data: 612,613 ovulatory cycles from 124,648 users.
  - Mean cycle length 29.3 days. Only 13% of cycles were exactly 28 days; 65% were 25–30 days and 19% were 31–35 days.
  - Mean follicular phase 16.9 days (95% CI 10–30); mean luteal phase 12.4 days (95% CI 7–17).
  - Average variation within one user's cycles: 2.6 days.
  - Between ages 25 and 45, cycle length shortened by 0.18 days a year. Users with BMI above 35 had 14% more cycle variation.
  - The authors conclude that women must understand their own cycle characteristics rather than rely on a standard cycle.
- Oura (JMIR; 2025 blog): ±1.26 days error against ±3.44 for calendar; 1.48 against 6.63 days in irregular cycles; 82% against 32.5% of estimates within 2 days. The study was done by the company. — [Oura blog, 3 Feb 2025](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-ovulation-detection-algorithm-validation-study/)
- Johnson, Marriott & Zinaman, *Current Medical Research and Opinion* (2018, PMID 29749274), compared ovulation predictions from apps and published calendar methods with the true probability of ovulation. Its quantitative results could not be retrieved. — [PubMed 29749274](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29749274/)
- What apps and users say about accuracy:
  - Spot On says its predictions are for "regular periods". — [Planned Parenthood Direct (search snippet)](https://www.plannedparenthooddirect.org/spot-on-period-tracker)
  - Glow reviews raise doubts about ovulation-prediction accuracy. — [Glow App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/glow-ovulation-period-app/id638021335)
  - Spot On reviews report inaccurate predictions with some contraceptive methods. — [Spot On App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/spot-on-period-tracker/id1039914781)
  - Users want apps that let them correct predictions and explain anomalies such as stress or birth-control changes. — [Mohan & Jenkins 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12131320/)
- What regulators require: the Swedish MPA asked NC to state the unwanted-pregnancy risk more clearly in-app, and the UK ASA ruled that "highly accurate" was misleading. — [Digital Health, 2018](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2018/09/swedish-authorities-natural-cycles-app-assessment/); [ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/naturalcycles-nordic-ab-sweden-a17-393896.html)

### Inferences
**How to communicate predictions honestly**
- **Show periods as ranges.** For example, "most likely 12–15 October", with the range widening as the user's cycle variability grows. Show the basis: "average 31 days, varies ±4 days over your last 6 cycles".
- **Admit low confidence.** For the first one or two cycles, or after big changes (stopping the pill, postpartum, perimenopause), say "not enough data yet" or "low confidence" instead of a precise date.
- **Never show a single ovulation day from calendar data alone.** Label the fertile window "estimated, not for contraception". Encourage optional markers (LH result, BBT, Apple Watch temperature) and explain that these improve the estimate and can confirm ovulation afterwards.
- **Avoid contraception-like visuals.** No "safe day" or green/red framing. In the EU, NC's CE Class IIb status shows that contraceptive claims make an app a regulated medical device; Turkish medical-device rules generally follow the EU framework (inferred, not verified).
- **Handle irregular cycles.** Let users annotate cycles (stress, illness, medication change), exclude outlier cycles from averages, and flag patterns worth discussing with a doctor (for example, repeated cycles over 35 days). Present these as educational cues, not diagnoses.
- **Explain how predictions work.** A plain-language explainer like drip's builds trust and sets expectations, and fits a privacy-first brand.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed comparison of *period-start* prediction accuracy across commercial apps in 2023–2026. Most studies cover ovulation and fertile windows.
- The quantitative results of Johnson et al. (2018) and Duane et al. (*JABFM* 2016, fertility-awareness apps for avoiding pregnancy) could not be retrieved.
- A widely repeated claim that "apps are ~21% accurate for fertile-window prediction (JAMA Internal Medicine 2020)" appeared only on low-quality aggregator pages. I could not trace it to a primary source, so it should not be used.

## 8. (From the objective) What lessons apply to a privacy-first, on-device app like Nilu (iOS, encrypted local storage, Turkey first)?

### Takeaway
The evidence points to a clear gap: a polished, Turkish-language, no-account, on-device tracker. It would offer Euki-level verifiable privacy, honest irregular-cycle predictions shown as ranges, optional manual BBT/LH logging plus on-device Apple Watch temperature, and a solid encrypted backup and migration path. It would make no contraception claims and use no ads or third-party SDKs. Competitors either do privacy well with weak product depth (Euki, drip, Spot On) or deep features with cloud accounts and privacy baggage (NC, Glow, Ovia, Premom).

### Cited Findings
- Architecture beats promises: Mozilla's 2026 summary says "Data minimization is stronger than a promise not to misuse data". Euki, with local storage and no account, scored 10/10, while apps with anonymous modes or SDKs leaked identifiers. — [Captain Compliance summary of Mozilla 2026](https://captaincompliance.com/education/mozilla-tested-six-period-tracker-apps-only-one-earned-a-perfect-privacy-score/)
- Hidden leaks come from SDKs and web views: Spot On's in-app browser sent location, age and last menstrual period to analytics vendors. 5 of 20 Android fertility apps leaked health data to ad services. — [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/education/mozilla-tested-six-period-tracker-apps-only-one-earned-a-perfect-privacy-score/); [arXiv 2606.26276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26276)
- Local apps lose data: Spot On reviews report data lost in updates, and Euki and drip support only manual migration. — [Spot On App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/spot-on-period-tracker/id1039914781); [netzpolitik.org](https://netzpolitik.org/2025/datenschutzfreundliche-perioden-apps-zyklus-tracking-ohne-tracking/)
- Safety features matter: Euki's decoy PIN shows a false screen and it has auto-delete. Around 56% of Turkish users worried others could see their device activity (2018). — [Mozilla blog 2022](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/privacy-not-included-spotlight-euki-on-how-it-gets-privacy-right/); [Memurlar.net (Kaspersky), 2018](https://www.memurlar.net/haber/762406/kullanicilarin-izleniyoruz-endisesi-giderek-artiyor.html?amp=1)
- Privacy alone does not create scale: Euki has 157 US App Store ratings and about 10k active users (2024), against about 75–91K ratings for Glow and Ovia. — [Euki App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/euki/id1469213846); [Ibis 2024](https://www.ibisreproductivehealth.org/news/euki-launches-dedicated-independent-501c3-nonprofit-tech-organization); [Glow App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/glow-ovulation-period-app/id638021335); [Ovia App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ovia-cycle-pregnancy-tracker/id570244389)
- Localization gap: NC supports English, Portuguese, Spanish and Swedish; Euki supports English and Spanish. — [NC App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/natural-cycles-birth-control/id765535549); [Euki App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/euki/id1469213846)
- Price anchors:
  - NC: $149.99 a year
  - Glow Premium: up to $69.99 a year
  - Moody Month: offers a lifetime purchase
  - WHOOP: bundles 12 months of NC to reach users through wearables

  — [NC App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/natural-cycles-birth-control/id765535549); [Glow App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/glow-ovulation-period-app/id638021335); [Moody Month App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1414905792); [FemTech Insider](https://femtechinsider.com/whoop-partners-with-natural-cycles-for-hormone-free-birth-control/)
- Users choose on transparency and security (ICO: 59% and 57%, above cost at 55%). They want accurate predictions for irregular cycles, encryption and biometric locks, and local-only options. — [ICO 2023](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2023/09/ico-to-review-period-and-fertility-tracking-apps); [Mohan & Jenkins 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12131320/)
- Markers improve accuracy: temperature-based detection beats calendar estimates, especially for irregular cycles. — [Oura validation](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-ovulation-detection-algorithm-validation-study/); [Worsfold et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8504278/)

### Inferences
1. **Make the privacy claim verifiable, not just stated.**
   - Ship with an App Store "Data Not Collected" label, like Euki.
   - Use no third-party analytics, crash or ad SDKs, and no in-app web views that load third-party scripts. Open links in Safari.
   - Publish a short technical privacy note covering encryption, the key held on the device, and the absence of a server.
   - Consider open-sourcing the prediction engine and seeking independent review (a Mozilla-style test or a security audit).
2. **Fix local-only pain points first.**
   - Encrypted export and import controlled by the user.
   - An optional end-to-end-encrypted backup, with the key derived on the device and never readable by Nilu.
   - A move-to-new-iPhone flow and protection against loss during app updates.
   - Import from Apple Health and from Flo/Clue exports, as drip does with converters.
3. **Safety and discretion as core features for the Turkish context.**
   - Face ID or PIN lock, and an optional decoy PIN that shows neutral data (as in Euki).
   - Neutral icon and notification text.
   - Sexual-activity logging hidden or off by default.
   - Quick "wipe all data".
4. **Honest predictions.** Use ranges with confidence levels, explicit handling of irregular cycles, user annotations and a plain-language explainer. Never use "safe days" or contraceptive framing.
5. **Optional advanced layer without regulatory exposure.**
   - Manual LH results (negative, high, peak) and BBT logging with charts for trying to conceive.
   - Apple Watch wrist temperature read through HealthKit on the device, used to confirm ovulation afterwards. Verify API availability.
   - Keep the existing pregnancy mode.
   - These serve Glow, Premom and Kindara-type users without camera AI or a cloud account.
6. **Business model consistent with privacy.** Glow, Ovia and Premom show the conflicts created by ads and data. Better options:
   - one-time purchase or modest subscription for advanced features, possibly with a lifetime tier (Moody Month)
   - partnerships with wearable or test makers that involve no data sharing
   - pricing well below NC, since there is no contraceptive claim
7. **Positioning for Turkey.**
   - Lead with "not even we can see your data", "no account", "not shared with advertisers" and "safe if someone picks up your phone", rather than US abortion-prosecution framing.
   - Mention KVKK alignment (health and sexual-life data are special-category data).
   - Treat EU users as a natural second market, where GDPR special-category status and regulator scrutiny (ICO, Cambridge) make data minimization a selling point.
8. **Content and community.** Prefer curated, offline, evidence-based Turkish content, with cautious cycle-phase wellness guidance, over server-based forums that would compromise the architecture.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect Nilu's current feature set; these lessons assume it as described (iOS, encrypted on-device storage, Turkey first).
- KVKK obligations for purely on-device apps and for optional encrypted iCloud backup need Turkish legal review.
- No Turkish-market data on willingness to pay for period apps or market share of Flo, Clue and local apps such as Pepapp was found in this research; another researcher may cover it.
